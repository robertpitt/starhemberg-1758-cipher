## English

**Provisional translation:** The whole text below translates the editorial working text, including its unverified passages. Unbracketed wording is not automatically authenticated. Continuous framing currently stops at P1-R05, with lexical gaps before that point.

On reading the enclosed […] ministerial letter, I have only this to observe respectfully: should the circumstance mentioned in it, concerning the [conduct of this court] at Constantinople, really prove true, it [would] be necessary to inform me [of it], so that I could make the official representation here.

Marshal Belle-Isle is busily making all arrangements for the speedy restoration of the army and the improvement of its existing military organisation. By all appearances he can expect his efforts to take good effect. He keeps assuring me that Soubise's corps will without fail arrive in Bohemia in July. But I do not yet dare to count on it fully: past experience has taught me only too well how little one can rely on the people here in military measures of this kind, especially when they are aimed at a somewhat distant date.

The Abbé de Bernis takes everything M. Durand has reported, about the intrigues between Count Brühl and the dismissed Russian Grand Chancellor, as certain and reliable truth. I suspect that Count Broglie will back Durand's claims as strongly as he can. Broglie seems to me to have more of a hearing with the Abbé than one would have thought. I expect him to do his utmost to move this court to make Count Brühl suspect to his King and to bring him down entirely. Count [Stainville] himself recommends this in a letter in his own hand to the Abbé [de] Bernis, part of which the minister read to me. This surprises me all the more, since Stainville otherwise seems to doubt somewhat whether Durand's report is entirely accurate, and in general speaks of the matter with great restraint.

The Abbé [de] Bernis has most solemnly repeated his promise to make a further payment of the subsidies. Whether it will actually be kept, I no longer dare to promise, since my assurances on this point have so often come to nothing. For my part I certainly don't let my constant and urgent representations and reminders slacken. I only wish that Count Stainville would also keep pressing on his court the need to keep to the stipulated payments more punctually than it has so far. But I suspect he would rather not mention this odious subject at all. He prefers to make himself agreeable to his court, and earn credit there, by obtaining favourable conditions and relief from the stipulations of the secret treaty.
